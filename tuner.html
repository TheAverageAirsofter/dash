Where these numbers come from

The controller never sends "speed". It sends a raw motor count, and speed is worked out from it: rpm = raw × 4 ÷ pole pairs, then km/h = rpm × wheel circumference × 60 ÷ 1000. That's why the two boxes above exist. Wrong pole pairs and your speed is off by a fixed multiple.

Power isn't sent either — it's volts × current. Negative current means the motor is pushing charge back into the pack under braking, so power goes negative and turns blue.

Volts, current, temperatures, charge and gear are read straight out of registers E8, EA, F4, DB, F5 and E2. Those six were already decoded by the community before you started. Everything else in the grid below is still unknown.

Why you have to do this yourself

Your controller has 256 slots of memory, each holding one number. A few are live readings, most are settings. Fardriver has never published which slot is which, and the people who reverse-engineered this protocol worked out the telemetry slots but not the tuning ones.

So you find them by watching. Snapshot records all 256 values. You change a setting using the official app — which does know the addresses — and compare. The slot that moved is your answer.

Live readings drift on their own, so several usually move at once. Anything flagged in the Known column is telemetry and can be ignored. If two unknowns moved, change the same setting again by a different amount and watch which one tracks it.

What you're looking at

The controller doesn't answer questions, it broadcasts. Every few milliseconds it sends a 16-byte frame carrying six registers, cycling through its memory. This grid fills in as frames arrive, which is why it starts empty and takes a few seconds to populate.

Values are 16-bit — 0000 to FFFF in hex, 0 to 65535 in ordinary numbers. Settings sit still. Live readings twitch constantly. That difference is itself a clue about which is which.

What naming a register actually does

Four things get stored. The name, so you stop thinking in hex. The limits — the lowest and highest raw values you'll ever let yourself write, which is your protection against a typo. The multiplier, for registers that store tenths, where raw 725 means 72.5 volts. And the unit label.

Export map saves it as a file. This is the valuable output of everything here: a verified map is what lets you build a real tuning app, and it's what you'd share with other riders on the same controller.

AA start marker · 46 means write, 8 bytes long · addr addr the address, sent twice as its own check · lo hi your value, low byte first · crc crc checksum
What happens when you press send, and what can go wrong

Eight bytes go out. Then the app waits for the controller to broadcast that register again and compares what came back against what you asked for. A match logs green. A mismatch logs red, meaning the controller refused or clamped your value — worth knowing. Nothing back within four seconds logs as unconfirmed, and you should assume it didn't take.

The lock, the limits and the read-back all protect against the same thing: a value that's wrong by accident. None of them protect against a correct value sent to the wrong register. The controller has no idea what you meant. Put a phase-current number in a line-current slot and it will accept it, run, and cook something.

So: wheel off the ground, nobody on the bike, one change at a time, until your map has proved itself over a few sessions.
